A research program at Van Lang University

No two people struggle the same way.

Most psychology studies tell us about the average person. We are more interested in the actual person in front of us.

In our studies, people answer a few short questions on their phone several times a day, for weeks or even months. From those answers we build a picture of how their own feelings, thoughts and habits push and pull on each other. We call that picture a person-specific network, and no two of them look alike.

How a study works

A few questions, a few times a day

Participants get a short survey on their phone, usually three or four times a day. Each one takes about a minute.

One network for each person

After a few months there are enough answers to see which feelings tend to lead to which, for that person alone.

Talking it through

We sit down with every participant, show them their own network and ask whether it matches how their life actually feels.

Projects

Four studies, four very different lives

Four funded studies, running from 2027 to 2029, and all four are recruiting participants now.

Each study follows a small group of people very closely instead of a large group briefly. That trade is deliberate. With enough answers from one person, we can say something about that person, which is exactly what large one-off surveys can't do. Every study has its own page with who we are looking for, what taking part involves, and a quick way to check whether it suits you.

Training

Learn the methods with us

We teach what we use. If these methods are going to grow in Vietnam, more people need a way to learn them properly.

Our course is free and non-profit, taught to small groups so that everyone gets real practice instead of watching from the back of a lecture hall. Alongside it, we are preparing two hands-on workshops on the tools our own studies depend on.

Course in two partsOngoing26PSM_01 and 26PSM_02Taught by Phan Trung Nguyen

Quantitative Research Methods for Psychological Science 1 & 2

A grounding in how psychological research actually gets done, with the weight on quantitative methods. We walk through the whole research process, from shaping a question and reading the literature, to choosing methods, collecting and analyzing data, and making sense of what you find. Ethics and a researcher's mindset run through all of it, meaning critical thinking, objectivity, curiosity and a willingness to follow the evidence.

By the end, students can read a study and judge how much to trust it, see the strengths and limits of different approaches, and start doing research of their own with more independence.

Why it is different

Statistics in psychology is often taught as a string of mathematical procedures, with little time spent on what the numbers mean. Many students come away feeling that quantitative methods have more to do with math than with people.

This course treats statistics as a set of tools for understanding psychological life. The focus is on interpreting results well, knowing where a method runs out, and being honest about what the numbers can and cannot tell us.

What it leaves out

No single course can cover everything, so we are upfront about the gaps.

  • Qualitative research. Apart from some philosophy of science, qualitative methods are not taught here, though they come up in discussion when the comparison helps.
  • Experimental design. Manipulating variables and running experiments are outside the scope.
  • Very specialized methods. We focus on the methods people actually use most, and skip the niche ones.

About

Why one person at a time

Averages are useful, but nobody is average.

A finding that holds for a group can say very little about the one person sitting across from a therapist. Maybe poor sleep leads to worry for one person, while for someone else it is the worry that ruins the sleep. A group average blends those two people into someone who doesn't exist.

If we ask the same person the same few questions many times, their own patterns start to show. This kind of research is still rare in Vietnam. We want to run the first local studies, share what we learn openly, and make the tools easy for other researchers to pick up.

How we work

Data
Short phone surveys, three or four times a day, over weeks or months
Analysis
Graphical vector autoregression, giving one network per person
Checks
Removing trends, testing stationarity and checking that each network holds up across both halves of the data
Openness
Every study is preregistered on OSF and reported with the FINA checklist
Afterward
A conversation with each participant about what their network shows

Common questions

What exactly is a person-specific network?

It is a map of one person's inner life over time. Each circle is something we ask about, such as mood or worry. Each arrow means that when one goes up, the other tends to follow a few hours later. Because it is built only from that person's answers, it describes them, not an average of everyone.

Why do some studies have only five or six people?

Because the person is the unit of study, not the group. Each participant answers a few hundred surveys, which is enough to estimate their own network reliably. Five or six people followed this closely tell us far more about how individuals work than a few hundred people surveyed once.

What is ecological momentary assessment?

It is the formal name for asking people short questions in the moment, during their normal day, instead of asking them to remember how they felt last week. Memory smooths things over. Asking in the moment catches the ups and downs as they happen.

How will participants' answers be protected?

Every study goes through ethics review before it starts. The details of how answers are stored and who can see them will be set out in each study's consent form, so people know exactly what they are agreeing to before they sign up.

Can I take part?

Yes. All four studies are recruiting now. Each study page explains who we are looking for and has a quick check to see whether it suits you. Write to us and we will send you the full details.
